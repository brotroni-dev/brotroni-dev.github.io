When the proposal enters the room

Turning proposals into a spatial experience clients can stand inside.

A proposal can be clear on paper and still be difficult to understand at full scale. Plans and renders showed clients the design, but still required them to translate a flat representation into the experience of standing inside the finished space. AR brought the proposal into the real room, giving pros and clients a shared spatial view they could react to together.

Role / Product Designer Scope / AR setup and alignment flow, interaction design, prototyping, on-device validation Platform / Mobile / AR

Context & problem

Clients still had to imagine the design. Plans and renders could show the proposal, but not how it would feel to be inside it.

Goal

Make the proposal feel real. Help pros present designs in context, so clients could understand, discuss, and trust the proposal faster - and reduce back-and-forth.

Why AR

As AR became more accessible and competitors brought it into planning workflows, we saw an opportunity to make Houzz Pro's floor plans more tangible and useful on site.

  • Meet market expectations - AR was becoming a more common part of spatial planning tools.
  • Bring plans into context - users could view the design inside the actual room, not only as a flat plan or render.
  • Support on-site conversations - pros could work through design decisions with clients on location.

The central tension

Believable, without becoming cumbersome. Precise alignment made the proposal feel credible, but every additional setup step pulled the pro away from the client conversation.

The experience had to feel spatially convincing without turning setup into a calibration task.

The design - a walkthrough

Turning that opportunity into a usable feature came down to one flow: getting the digital plan to line up with the real room - convincingly, and in as few steps as possible.

The AR flow connected the plan to the room. Pros launched AR from the floor plan list, chose a starting point, aligned the digital plan with the real space, and previewed the design in AR.

01Aligning the floor plan with the real space

Getting the design to sit correctly in the room was the central challenge of the flow. The model is how I approached it: anchoring to where the user stood and which way they faced, then rotating the plan to match.

I designed the alignment model around a fixed user position, allowing the digital plan to rotate until it matched the user's real-world view. Geolocation helped establish the broader context, while the position marker and viewing direction supported more precise alignment.

  • Viewing direction - where the user is facing.
  • User position - fixed anchor in the real space.
  • Plan rotation - matches the layout to the room.
02Fitting the virtual room to the physical space

Once the starting point was aligned, users scanned the room and adjusted the virtual walls to match the physical space.

Set starting point → Scan the room → Fit virtual walls → Preview in AR.

Validation

Because scale, alignment, and spatial trust are difficult to evaluate in static prototypes, we tested the experience on-device and in real rooms throughout development.

  • On-device tests - orientation, alignment, and recovery issues that static screens couldn't reveal.
  • User sessions - checked whether people understood the setup and trusted the spatial result.
  • Client demos - observed whether AR supported the conversation instead of interrupting it.
  • Post-launch monitoring - tracked whether the feature was being adopted in real project work.

The proposal became a shared spatial reference

Pros and clients could discuss the design together, standing in the room.

Instead of asking clients to imagine the space, AR gave both sides one spatial view to react to - moving the conversation from translation to decision.

What the AR work taught me

Spatial experiences live or die on a careful balance between believability and effort.

01

Aligned enough to be believable

The result has to feel aligned enough with the real room for clients to trust it.

02

The setup can't become the experience

Precision matters, but the calibration can't take over and become the experience itself.

03

Ask, then get out of the way

Ask for only the input needed to place the proposal convincingly, then step back so the conversation stays on the design.
