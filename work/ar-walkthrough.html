When the proposal enters the room

Turning proposals into a spatial experience clients can stand inside.

A proposal can be clear on paper and still be difficult to grasp at full scale. Overview

Turning proposals into spatial presentations

Help pros present proposed designs as a spatial experience, reduce back-and-forth, and build client confidence faster.

Problem

Clients still had to imagine the design

Plans and renders could show the proposal, but clients still had to imagine how it would feel inside the actual space.

Goal

Make the proposal feel real

Help pros present designs in context, so clients could understand, discuss, and trust the proposal faster.

Why AR

Why AR?

Plans and renders communicated the idea, but clients still had to imagine it in the actual space. As AR became more accessible and competitors brought it into planning workflows, we saw an opportunity to make Houzz Pro's floor plans more tangible and useful on site.

01

Meet market expectations

AR was becoming a more common part of spatial planning tools.

02

Bring plans into context

Users could view the design inside the actual room, not only as a flat plan or render.

03

Support on-site conversations

Pros could discuss design decisions with clients while standing in the space itself.

The flow

The AR flow connected the plan to the room

Pros launched AR from the floor plan list, chose a starting point, aligned the digital plan with the real space, and previewed the design in AR.

Alignment model

Aligning the floor plan with the real space

Getting the design to sit correctly in the room was the central challenge of the flow. I designed the alignment model around a fixed user position, letting the digital plan rotate until it matched the user's real-world view. Geolocation established the broader context, while the position marker and viewing direction supported more precise alignment.

On device

Fitting the virtual room to the physical space

Once the starting point was aligned, users scanned the room and adjusted the virtual walls to match the physical space.

The tradeoff

The core tradeoff: believable, without becoming cumbersome

Precise alignment made the proposal feel credible. But every additional setup step pulled the pro away from the client conversation. We needed the minimum calibration required for a stable, convincing result.

01 · Believable

  • Match the user's real position and viewing direction
  • Fit virtual walls to the physical room
  • Keep the proposal stable at room scale

02 · Lightweight

  • Avoid complex map controls
  • Minimize repeated calibration
  • Make repositioning easy
Validation

Validation

Because scale, alignment, and spatial trust are difficult to evaluate in static prototypes, we tested the experience on-device and in real rooms throughout development.

01

On-device tests

Orientation, alignment, and recovery issues that static screens couldn't reveal.

02

Checked whether people understood the setup and trusted the spatial result.

03

Client demos

Observed whether AR supported the conversation instead of interrupting it.

04

Post-launch monitoring

Tracked whether the feature was being adopted in real project work.

Outcome

The proposal became a shared spatial reference

Instead of asking clients to imagine the design from a plan, AR gave pros and clients something they could inspect, react to, and discuss together in the room.

The conversation moved from translation to decision - both sides looking at one spatial view, standing in the space itself.

Learnings

What the AR work taught me

Spatial experiences live or die on a careful balance between believability and effort.

01

Aligned enough to be believed

The result has to feel aligned enough with the real room for clients to trust it.

02

Setup can't become the experience

Precision matters, but calibration can't take over and become the experience itself.

03

Ask, then get out of the way

Ask only for the input needed to place the proposal convincingly, then step back so the conversation stays on the design.
